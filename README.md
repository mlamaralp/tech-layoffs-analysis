# What Drives Larger Tech Layoffs?

Analysing 2,412 tech layoff events (2020-2025) to understand whether financial maturity and capital availability predict layoff severity, and how the 2022-2023 wave compared across industries and funding stages.

---

## Research Question

**What structural and financial characteristics of tech companies are associated with larger proportional layoffs?**

Two hypotheses are tested:

- **H1:** Companies that raised more capital will cut a smaller share of their workforce
- **H2:** Layoff severity differs significantly across funding stages (Seed to Post-IPO)

## Methodology

### Data Cleaning

`Date_layoffs` was parsed to datetime and `Year` and `YearMonth` were extracted. A log1p transformation was applied to `Money_Raised_in__mil` because the variable is heavily right-skewed: a small number of companies raised hundreds of billions while the median is around $160M. Without the transformation, the regression would be driven by a handful of outliers. Each analysis was performed on complete cases for the relevant variables, with no imputation, to avoid introducing artificial variance.

### Method 1: Spearman Correlation + OLS Regression

Tests whether companies with more capital tend to cut a smaller share of their workforce (H1).

**Why Spearman and not Pearson?** Both `Money_Raised` and `Percentage` are strongly right-skewed even after log-transformation. Spearman works on ranks rather than raw values, so it is not sensitive to the shape of the distribution or to extreme values. Pearson assumes approximate normality and would give misleading results here.

**Why add OLS on top of Spearman?** Spearman only tells us whether an association exists and in which direction. OLS provides a quantifiable estimate of the effect size: specifically, how many percentage points of workforce reduction are associated with each unit increase in log(capital raised). The two methods are complementary: Spearman for robustness, OLS for interpretability.

**Why a bootstrap CI for Spearman?** The standard asymptotic p-value for Spearman assumes large samples and approximate normality of the test statistic. A bootstrap confidence interval makes no such assumption. It directly estimates the sampling distribution of rho by resampling the data 1,000 times, which is a more honest way to quantify uncertainty with skewed data.

### Method 2: Kruskal-Wallis Test

Tests whether layoff severity (% of workforce cut) differs significantly across funding stages (H2).

**Why Kruskal-Wallis and not ANOVA?** ANOVA assumes that the outcome variable is approximately normally distributed within each group and that variances are roughly equal across groups. The `Percentage` variable is bounded between 0 and 100, right-skewed, and the group sizes are very unequal (e.g. 574 Post-IPO events vs 49 Seed events). Kruskal-Wallis is the non-parametric equivalent of ANOVA: it tests whether the distributions differ across groups without any assumption about their shape.

**Why group the stages?** The original dataset has 16 distinct funding stages, several with very small sample sizes (Series H: 27, Series I: 8, Series J: 6). Running a test with 16 groups would produce unreliable estimates for the smaller groups and make the results hard to interpret. Grouping into five meaningful categories (Early: Seed-A, Growth: B-D, Late: E+, Post-IPO, and Acquired/Private Equity) reflects real differences in business maturity and ensures each group has enough observations.

**Why add pairwise Mann-Whitney tests?** Kruskal-Wallis only tells us that at least one group differs from the others. Pairwise Mann-Whitney U tests identify exactly which stage groups differ significantly from each other. Bonferroni correction is applied to account for the increased probability of false positives when running 10 comparisons at once.

## Key Findings

| Metric | Value | Interpretation |
|--------|-------|----------------|
| Spearman rho | -0.316 | Negative association: more capital, smaller cuts |
| p-value (Spearman) | < 0.001 | Statistically significant |
| Bootstrap 95% CI | [-0.361, -0.274] | CI excludes zero |
| OLS beta | -4.85 | Each log-unit increase in capital is associated with ~4.8 pp fewer cuts |
| OLS R2 | 0.104 | Capital explains about 10% of variance in layoff severity |
| Kruskal-Wallis H | 277.92 | Strong group differences |
| p-value (K-W) | < 0.001 | Highly significant across all stage groups |

Financial maturity acts as a buffer: well-funded and mature companies cut proportionally less. But with R2 around 10%, capital is a signal rather than the dominant driver. The 2022-2023 systemic shock affected virtually all stages and sectors regardless of financial position.

---

## Limitations

- **Selection bias:** only publicly reported layoffs are captured; small private events are underrepresented
- **Causality:** observational design; reverse causality is plausible (healthier companies both raise more and lay off less)
- **Proxy quality:** `Money_Raised` is a crude proxy for financial health at the time of the layoff; a runway measure would be more precise
- **Stage timing:** the recorded funding stage may not reflect the company's actual financial position at the time of the event
